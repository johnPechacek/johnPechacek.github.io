# Hunt Planning Research

**Role:** Product Designer
**Timeline:** September 2025 to January 2026
**Team:** Design Team, Cross-Functional Workshop Participants

**Outcome:**
A multi-method research project into how hunters plan their hunts, culminating in Moultrie's first cross-functional design workshop. The research reframed how the team understands the hunt planning problem. The findings are actively shaping a major product initiative currently in development.

---

## Problem

Moultrie already had tools for hunt planning. There was a Game Plan feature that combined activity forecasts with property maps. There was a wind tool. There were property lines overlaid on satellite imagery. But usage told a different story. Hunters weren't finding these features, and the ones who found them often didn't stick with them.

Meanwhile, hunters were juggling apps. Weather from one, wind forecasts from another, their camera feed from Moultrie, scouting notes somewhere else. The data they needed to plan a hunt existed. Getting it in one place didn't.

I was asked to understand why the planning features weren't landing, and what a better version of hunt planning could look like.

---

## Existing Research

I started by figuring out what Moultrie already knew. I went through dozens of past research projects looking for anything relevant to hunt planning.

One survey of 216 hunters had mapped out the basic shape of how hunters think about their time. 83% hunt 2 to 5 days at a time. Family commitments and work schedule are the top constraints on when they can go out. Hunters are frequently making the best of whatever window they have, not waiting for a perfect scenario. That framing matters for anything we'd build.

---

## Competitive Analysis

In September I went deep on competing apps: HuntStand, DeerCast, Spartan Forge, and Windy.app, among others.

The pattern across all of them was the same problem stated differently: wind is where the most important design decisions in this category live. HuntStand's per-stand wind analysis gives hunters a clear, trustworthy read on conditions across multiple upcoming days. That was the bar Moultrie was being measured against. The data Moultrie offered was there. The trust and readability were not.

---

## User Interviews

In October and into November I ran 8 interviews, five virtual and three in person, each about an hour. The people I talked to ranged from casual weekend hunters to people who run hunt clubs and teach others to hunt. All of them juggled at least two apps as a normal part of their routine.

One hunter put it plainly: "I wish I didn't have to use OnX for property lines, HuntStand for wind and Moultrie for pictures." He wasn't complaining about any one app. He was describing the coordination tax that had become normal.

Hunt planning also operates on two timescales. One hunter described checking the general forecast early in the week just to rule out terrible conditions, then shifting entirely to hourly wind forecasts and specific stands by Thursday or Friday. The same person with two different sets of needs depending on where they were in the week. An app that presents a single planning experience isn't serving either moment particularly well.

The frustrations I heard were consistent: insufficient wind tools, having to leave the app for a reliable forecast, a camera gallery that was hard to manage at scale, and no good way to do bigger mapping work on a laptop. Several hunters had no idea Game Plan existed. Property lines were unknown. The features with the most potential value were invisible to people who would have used them.

---

## Survey

I ran the survey in December 2025 and collected 151 responses.

Going into it, I expected the data to reflect what I'd heard from several hunters who drove significant distances to their land. If travel time was a major factor, I assumed it would push planning farther out. The results went the other direction. Most hunters live within an hour of where they hunt. The longer-commute stories from the interviews were outliers, not the norm.

The bigger driver turned out to be schedule. 50% say free time or availability is what makes them decide to go out on a given day. Another 26% pointed to camera activity. 39% decide 1 to 3 days in advance. Another 8% decide the same day.

The interviews had also made clear how fluid specific hunting plans are. A hunter might commit to going out days in advance, but which stand they sit and whether they go at all can shift right up until morning, almost always because of wind. That led me to recommend adaptable planning tools over fixed itinerary-style features, something that could update with conditions rather than require replanning from scratch.

When hunters rank what information matters most for planning, camera activity comes first at a mean rank of 2.3, with weather forecast close behind at 2.4. Wind direction ranked first for weather by a wide margin, at a mean of 1.9 compared to wind speed at 3.1. A feature that requires navigating to a separate section and reading a chart the user hasn't learned to trust is not going to be part of that decision.

---

## Concepts

In early December I synthesized the research and put together concept designs to bring into the leadership presentation. These were single high-fidelity screens intended to spark discussion rather than specify a direction. The concept I focused on was a per-property dashboard: a view that surfaced the highest-impact planning information for a specific property at a glance. Buck activity, wind direction, camera highlights, when a stand was last hunted. A hunter opening the app the night before a hunt could see what they need to make a confident decision without navigating across multiple features to find it.

In mid-December I presented the full research to leadership. The discussion that followed was useful. The more interesting work was about to happen.

---

## The Workshop

I had been pushing for a design workshop since earlier in the project. My case to my manager had two parts. The first was about the problem. We had clear research on how hunters think and plan, and I thought a cross-functional group working through it from different angles would find directions the design team wouldn't arrive at on its own. The second was about the team's capability. We hadn't run a workshop before, and this project had the right conditions to learn how to do it well. He agreed.

We organized the session over several weeks. My specific contribution to the preparation was writing the three personas that teams would use throughout the day, each a synthesis of the interview and survey findings.

On January 14, 2026, 13 people came together for Moultrie's first cross-functional design workshop. The group spanned product management, development, marketing, QA, and customer experience, with a mix of leadership and junior team members. My manager facilitated from outside the teams. Another designer and I each joined one of the three teams to answer questions and keep things moving.

Teams worked through empathy mapping, as-is journey maps, big idea generation, and force ranking before landing on paper prototyping. One team's prototype stood out. They had traced the full arc of a hunter's relationship with a property, not just the days before a hunt, but the months between seasons when the property sits mostly unvisited. Their concept described an experience where the app would help hunters plan the physical placement of stands and blinds using camera data and map information. An offseason tool. Something the current app doesn't address at all.

After the workshop I wrote a feedback survey and sent it to all participants. 75% strongly agreed the session had been valuable. 88% said they'd want to participate again. Two themes came up in the written responses: the scope had drifted from hunt planning toward the broader Moultrie ecosystem, and people wanted a clearer path from the ideas generated to something actionable on a roadmap.

---

## What Came Next

Leadership made a bigger bet than what I had recommended. There is a major product initiative currently in development that I'm not in a position to describe publicly.

What I can say is what the research produced. Coming out of this project, the team has a much clearer working model of how hunters actually plan. We understand what their goals are at different phases in their hunt planning journey, why most of them are working within a much shorter planning window than I initially assumed, and why the final decision almost always comes down to wind and recent camera activity. That framing has been the reference point in planning discussions for the new initiative since the research concluded.

The offseason opportunity that surfaced in the workshop is also part of that conversation. One of the session's prototypes is confirmed for the new project.

The key decision-maker at the company was on one of those three teams. He spent the day doing the work alongside everyone else: mapping journeys, generating ideas, building a prototype from a persona. The offseason opportunity didn't come from a slide. He found it himself.

---

## Reflection

This project didn't produce a feature recommendation that got built. It produced something more durable: a clear picture of what hunters are actually doing and where the experience fails them. That framing survived a pivot.

The workshop taught me something I hadn't fully expected. Getting a cross-functional group to physically work through a user's journey together produces a different kind of understanding than a presentation does. The offseason insight didn't come from my interviews or my survey. It came from a team following a persona through a journey map and asking what happens after the season ends. I could have asked that question in an interview. I don't think it would have carried the same weight in the room.

The feedback pointed to two things I'd fix before running one of these again. First, scope: the session drifted from hunt planning specifically toward the broader Moultrie ecosystem, and tighter exercise constraints would help with that. Second, the jump from an exciting prototype to an actionable next step was the gap people felt most. A closing activity that helps teams prioritize ideas and identify a clear owner would go a long way.
