This is earlier work from Istation, which makes reading assessment and instruction software for schools. The students it was for were 3 to 5 years old and couldn't read yet. Every instruction, label and piece of feedback had to work without words, which is why we tested with a child instead of guessing.

---

## Problem

Istation's Spanish curriculum had a gap in its Pre-K standards coverage. It was missing phonological awareness, the ability to hear and work with the sounds in spoken words, which children need before they can learn to read.

The content had to teach real skills, hold the attention of 3 year olds, and be usable by students who couldn't read a single instruction or label.

---

## Starting From the Classroom

The team's educational experts identified the foundational skills students needed and showed us how teachers already teach them. For counting syllables, students place a counter on a card for each syllable they hear, left to right.

  <figcaption>Classroom strategies the educational experts shared. We started every activity from these.</figcaption>

I led the brainstorming sessions with rough sketches, working with the whole team to translate those classroom strategies to a screen.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/pa-sketches.webp" loading="lazy" alt="Three rough marker sketches of activity concepts: puzzle pieces building a picture, a character beside a row of counters and a check button, and a train carrying syllable cars" />
  <figcaption>Sketches from the team brainstorming sessions.</figcaption>
</figure>

Six principles came out of those sessions and guided every activity:

- Activities are brief and efficient.
- Art uses bright colors and curvy shapes.
- Every activity shares a single design system.
- Layouts and interactions stay simple.
- The UI is understandable without labels.
- Activities are replayable, so students can practice.

## Testing With Pre-Readers

I built low-fidelity prototypes in Adobe XD so we could test early and change designs before anyone wrote production code.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/pa-xd-prototypes.webp" loading="lazy" alt="Adobe XD prototype flows for three versions of the Counting Syllables activity, labeled plusMinus, disabledStates and Version 1, with correct and incorrect answer states" />
  <figcaption>Three versions of Counting Syllables, prototyped in Adobe XD with correct and incorrect answer states.</figcaption>
</figure>

The team felt it was critical to test with a pre-reader. We were only able to test with one student, but as a native Spanish speaker of Pre-K age, she was exactly who the activities were for. We paid her in pizza, and she was thrilled to help.

We tested three versions of the Counting Syllables activity.

## A Reason to Keep Practicing

The last principle, replayability, needed a reason for kids to come back. I designed a reward system where students collect friendly characters, then choose one to accompany them through the activities. The companion offers help when a student gets stuck.

The team's lead artist drew the characters. I vectorized them and built a puppet rig with motion design animations to plug every character into, and the team's developer put the rig and assets together as unlockable characters. Costume variants on the shared rig added up to hundreds of unlockable characters.

## Reflection

The most intuitive version of Counting Syllables taught the wrong habit. Testing showed us what kids found easy, and our curriculum specialists knew what would actually help them learn to read. I strongly advocate for early testing with target users, even when they're hard to test with. One pre-reader caught problems no adult reviewer would have, like not knowing to tap a counter again to remove it.

If I did it again, I'd push to test in a real classroom. Pre-readers rely on their teachers, and we never got to see how teachers guide students through activities like these.

I'd also fix a few UI decisions. Counting Syllables shows right and wrong answers mostly with red and green, when it should have used the check and X icons we used in other activities. The activity where students build words from spoken syllables needed the most work, and it's one we never got to test. Its play buttons didn't look draggable, and the only difference between them was color. I'd turn each syllable into a little creature, so kids could start to recognize the sounds by who was saying them.
